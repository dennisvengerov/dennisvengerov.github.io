Project 1

Images of the Russian Empire

Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii Photo Collection

Normalized cross-correlation

To score a candidate shift I compare the interior of the shifted channel, A, with the interior of blue, B. I subtract each array's mean, a = A − mean(A) and b = B − mean(B), and compute

NCC = Σ ai bi / ( √Σ ai² · √Σ bi² )

where i runs over corresponding pixels. The numerator is the dot product of the two centered arrays and the denominator is the product of their lengths, so the score is the cosine of the angle between them and lies between −1 and 1. Subtracting the mean and dividing by the length make the score ignore differences in overall brightness and contrast between channels. If either array is constant its length is zero, and I return 0 for that candidate so it cannot win.

I also implemented the L2 distance,

L2 = √ Σ (ai − bi)²

where the shift with the smallest distance wins. It selects the same shifts as NCC, within one pixel, on every image except church and emir, where it fails, so all results below use NCC.

Single-scale alignment

Each scan holds the blue, green and red exposures stacked top to bottom. I convert it to floats between 0 and 1, cut it into three equal-height arrays, keep blue fixed, and search for the shift that moves green, and separately red, onto it. At the original resolution I try every shift from −15 to +15 pixels in both directions, 31 × 31 = 961 candidates per channel. For each candidate I roll the whole moving channel, crop 10 percent from every side of both it and blue, and compute NCC on the two central regions. The margin keeps the plate's black and white borders out of the score, and it also discards the pixels that roll wraps from one edge to the other. The candidate with the highest score is the shift, written (dx, dy) for columns moved right and rows moved down. Each JPG scan takes under a second.

Pyramid alignment

The TIF scans are about 3200 × 3700 pixels per channel and their displacements exceed 100 pixels, so a single-scale window large enough to contain them would need tens of thousands of candidates, each scored over millions of pixels. Instead I search on a small version of the image and refine on larger ones.

To halve an image I blur it with a Gaussian of σ = 1 pixel, built by hand as a seven-tap kernel and applied as two separable one-dimensional passes, then keep every other row and column. The blur removes the detail the smaller grid cannot represent, so it does not turn into false structure. The alignment function halves both channels recursively until the smaller side is below 300 pixels, about 200 pixels for the TIFs, and runs the ±15 single-scale search there. Each level above doubles the shift found below, since one pixel there is two pixels here, and searches ±2 around that estimate, 25 candidates, to correct rounding. The final shift is applied to the original green and red arrays. The same limits, ±15 at the coarsest level and ±2 above, are used for every image, and a TIF takes three to four seconds.

Additional images

I ran the same algorithm with the same parameters on four more plates from the Library of Congress collection.

Alignment failure

Emir's red channel is misaligned. The algorithm selects (24, 49) for green and (−205, 141) for red, which leaves a red copy of the figure to the left of him. The Emir's robe is bright in the blue exposure and dark in the red one, and the doors behind him do the opposite. Mean subtraction and normalization handle a global difference in brightness or contrast, but not a pattern that is inverted region by region, so NCC on raw pixels scores a wrong overlap higher than the correct one at the coarsest level, and the ±2 refinement at each larger level cannot recover from it. L2 lands closer, at (57, 103), but is still visibly off. Aligning on gradients instead of intensities would avoid the problem.
