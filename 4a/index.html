Overview

In this project, I wanted to understand how photos taken from the same position can be combined into a wider image. I started by clicking corresponding points, used them to recover a homography H, and then warped and blended the images together. I implemented these steps myself because I wanted to understand what actually happens to the pixels throughout the process. I did not use homography or warping functions from cv2 or skimage. For the supporting computations, I used numpy for linear algebra (lstsq, inv) and scipy for the distance transform that determines the blending weights.

I resized every photo to 1200 px on its longer side, giving images of size 900 by 1200. All pixel measurements on this page refer to these resized images.

I took three sets of photos for the mosaics and two additional photos for rectification in A.3. I wanted the images in each set to be related by a projective transform. For scenes with objects at different depths, this means rotating the camera while keeping its position fixed. I stayed in one place and turned my phone between shots, keeping about half of each image overlapping with the next.

I arranged each set below from left to right to match its order in the final mosaic. For the sets with a middle photo, I used that image as the reference. For the library pair, I used the right photo.

Part A.2

Recovering homographies

A homography describes how a point (x, y) in one image maps to a corresponding point (x′, y′) in another. I represent this transformation in homogeneous coordinates using a 3 by 3 matrix H. Fixing the bottom right entry to 1 leaves 8 unknown values, a through h, that I need to recover from the correspondences.

wx′wy′w = abcdefgh1 xy1

The matrix multiplication gives homogeneous coordinates. To recover the actual image coordinates, I divide by w:

x′ = (ax + by + c) / (gx + hy + 1), y′ = (dx + ey + f) / (gx + hy + 1)

Multiplying both sides by the denominator lets me rearrange each correspondence into two equations that are linear in a through h. I then stack the equations from all n point pairs into the system A h = b:

x1y11000−x1x′1−y1x′1 000x1y11−x1y′1−y1y′1 ⋮ xnyn1000−xnx′n−ynx′n 000xnyn1−xny′n−yny′n abcdefgh = x′1y′1⋮x′ny′n

The resulting matrix A has dimensions 2n by 8. With 4 suitably placed points, there are just enough equations to solve for the homography. However, my clicks can be a few pixels away from the actual corresponding locations, so relying on only 4 points makes the result sensitive to those errors. I used 9 points per image pair, with 7 for the library. The larger sets give 18 equations for 8 unknowns, which I solve using least squares with np.linalg.lstsq.

Recovered homographies

I wanted to understand what these values meant beyond just using them to warp the images. The translation term c is negative for photos to the left of the reference and positive for photos to its right. The perspective term g also changes sign between the left and right photos. It appears in the denominator gx + hy + 1, which increases with x for a left photo and decreases with x for a right photo. This helps explain why the outer edge of each side photo stretches more than the edge closer to the reference, producing the bow-tie shape visible in A.4.

To check how well each H fits the correspondences, I transform the clicked points from image 1 and measure their distance from the matching points in image 2.

Part A.3

Warping and rectification

I implemented inverse warping by starting with the output image. For each output pixel, I apply the inverse of H to find its location in the source image and read the color there. This gives every pixel within the warped image’s footprint a source location to sample. Forward warping works in the opposite direction, but pushing source pixels into the output can leave gaps between the locations where they land.

To determine the output dimensions, I transform the 4 image corners through H and use the minimum and maximum coordinates as the bounds. I also create an alpha mask with a value of 1 wherever the mapped point falls inside the source image and 0 elsewhere. This mask tells the blending procedure in A.4 which pixels are valid.

The mapped source coordinates usually fall between pixels. I implemented two methods for deciding which color to use at those locations.

Nearest neighbor. I round x and y to the nearest pixel coordinates and copy the color from that location.

Bilinear. I use the 4 pixels surrounding the source location and combine their colors based on the point’s position between them.

I implemented both interpolation methods directly with numpy indexing, without using griddata or map_coordinates.

Rectification

Before building the mosaics, I tested the warping code on objects that I knew were rectangular. I selected their 4 corners in order: top left, top right, bottom right, and bottom left. I then matched these points to the corners of a rectangle in the output image. This uses the same computeH function with 4 correspondences, followed by warping. A successful result should make the object look as though I photographed it from directly in front.

For these examples, I already know the rectangle I want to produce, so I restrict the output to that region. Warping the entire tilted photo could create an extremely large output because points near the line mapped to infinity would end up very far away.

Nearest neighbor vs. bilinear

The difference between the methods is difficult to see when viewing the complete images. To look more closely, I took 100 by 100 px crops of the outputs and displayed them without additional smoothing.

Quality. The smile and the letter Z show the difference most clearly. With nearest neighbor, each output pixel copies a single source pixel, which creates visible steps along the edges. Bilinear interpolation combines 4 neighboring pixels, making those edges smoother at the cost of some sharpness.

Speed. Bilinear interpolation requires 4 pixel lookups and 4 weights for each output pixel, while nearest neighbor needs only 1 lookup. This additional work made bilinear 1.4 to 2.5 times slower in these measurements. Both implementations use vectorized numpy operations and still finish in under half a second. A single run is not enough to establish a precise timing ratio, but the results are consistent with the extra computation bilinear requires.

Trade-off. I would use nearest neighbor for a quick preview or when reducing computation matters more than the appearance of the edges. For these final images, the additional time for bilinear interpolation is small enough that I prefer its smoother results. I therefore used bilinear interpolation for every mosaic.

Part A.4

Blending into mosaics

I build each mosaic around a reference photo and warp the remaining images directly into its coordinate system. Once the images share the same frame, I can place them on a common canvas and combine their overlapping regions. I use the following procedure:

  1. Warp. I transform each non-reference photo using its H and bilinear interpolation. I also pass the reference through the same function with the identity matrix. This gives me a consistent output for every image: its pixels, alpha mask, and offset.
  2. Canvas. I find the combined bounds of the warped images, starting at the smallest offset and extending to the largest corner coordinates. This lets me allocate the complete canvas before placing any image on it.
  3. Weights. I apply a distance transform to each image’s alpha mask to determine its blending weights. The weight reflects how far a pixel is from the nearest boundary, with 0 at the edge and larger values toward the interior.
  4. Average. I accumulate each image’s weighted colors and its weights on the canvas. Dividing the accumulated colors by the total weight gives the final pixel values.

Simply placing one photo over another would make any difference in exposure or color visible at the boundary. The distance-based weights help make that transition gradual. As an image approaches its edge, its contribution falls to 0 and the overlapping image contributes more. Areas outside all of the images remain black.

The most important thing I learned

As someone who is highly mathematical, I mostly focused on the Homography math. I learned about mapping points to different coordinate systems with matrixes. It is cool how we can represent a rotation and projection with our h matrix. Thus if we have x,y points, each one gives two equations added to the matrix eq. We get a linear system that we can solve analytically for the matrix, in my opinion that is really cool. I wonder if our brains learned some h matrix to align the images we see from each eye separately.
